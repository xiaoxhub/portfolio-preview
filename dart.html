DART Academy

The problem

$7.7 billionin reported internet crime losses among Americans 60 and older in 2025, more than any other age group (FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center)

Older adults face serious financial risks from scams, but prevention advice alone doesn’t prepare them for the moment a scam happens. Differences in memory, attention, vision, hearing, and confidence with technology can also affect how learners navigate online lessons, understand the content, and put it into practice.

The challenge was to help older adults recognize and respond to scams through a learning experience they could navigate with confidence at their own pace. That meant clear, accessible content, opportunities to practice in realistic situations, and a design that built on their life experience and desire to stay mentally active.

Who it’s for

  • Older adults learning independently, including those with limited technology experience.
  • Educators, librarians, and community workers who teach scam prevention and need materials they can use in workshops.
  • Potential partner organizations, such as financial advisory firms, that want to help their clients recognize and avoid scams.

Understanding these audiences was part of my research. I led interviews and user studies, while our user-testing team ran focus groups and workshops. As technical lead for the team’s 2025 NSF I-Corps program, I conducted about 40 customer-discovery interviews with financial advisors, educators, librarians, and community workers to understand how they help people avoid scams and how this course could reach the people they serve.

What the interviews showed. We had heard that many financial advisors’ clients are older adults who get scammed, and that some can’t be talked out of it, so we went to a financial advisors’ conference to test whether firms would pay for a course. Other vendors there offered scam-prevention presentations and phone tools that block or answer suspicious messages, so the industry already saw the need. Older adults might not pay for a course themselves, but a firm might buy one as a service for its clients; advisors called it a good idea and said they might consider it or recommend it, but no firm committed, so whether firms would pay is still untested. The closest comparable course we found, from a startup working with banks, was mostly narrated slides. That told us practice could set DART apart; it doesn’t by itself show that practice teaches better. The clearest adoption signal came later, from senior centers and a university instructor who asked to use the courses (see Delivery and early signals).

Scope

Version one covers self-paced online learning and in-person workshops led by educators, librarians, and community workers.

What version one includes: eight courses. Five full-length courses cover grandparent scams, tech support scams, medication scams, romance and friendship scams, and identity theft. Three 30-minute mini-courses cover gift card scams, IRS scams, and strong passwords. Learners who finish a course receive a certificate they can share by social media or text.

Why this scope: The project’s faculty leads chose the topics from FTC data on the scams older adults report. I then decided each topic’s format, based on how much it involves, and walked the faculty leads through the reasoning. Romance and friendship scams unfold over weeks and use many tactics, so that course runs seven modules. Creating a strong password or spotting an IRS impersonator can be learned in one short sitting, so those became 30-minute mini-courses.

Release order: Using the same FTC data, we decided which topics mattered most. Grandparent scams target older adults directly, and identity theft is the most common and affects people of every age, so those came first. I ran production as a pipeline: my team finished and internally tested one course, handed it to the engineer, and started the next while he built it. When funding was cut, all five full-length courses were already live.

What we chose not to require:

  • An account to start learning. Learners can open any course without signing in. An optional account saves their progress.
  • A passing score. Each course ends with an evaluation, but it is for learners to check themselves, not a pass-or-fail test. A low-pressure self-check lets learners see what they missed without the risk of failing, so the certificate comes from finishing the course, not from a score.

Built but not released: handed off for development, but not published before funding was cut.

  • Ten more mini-courses. I delivered five full-length courses and thirteen mini-courses. All five full-length courses and three mini-courses are live. The other ten mini-courses were not released, including two on deepfake scams that use fake voices and video.
  • Separately, for a second DART project: a library of phishing practice scenarios with feedback, also unreleased.

How it works

The learning experience

Every course follows five stages informed by experiential learning: Challenge, Learn, Practice, Evaluate, and Reflect. The Identity Theft course shows how I built them out. A pre-quiz activates what learners already know. Each of three short chapters pairs the content with a 1–3 question quiz and a reflection prompt that asks learners to connect it to their own experience. Simulated scam scenarios then ask them to use it. A final self-check has no pass/fail, and a closing review lets learners see their results and revisit any stage.

Finishing a course earns a certificate, and learners earn points and move up in rank along the way.

Yellow steps are testing and revision.

Reviewed and tested throughout development, through five different kinds of checks:

  • Expert review: three rounds on the first design, each with six or seven experts in gerontology, psychology, digital communication, and cybersecurity.
  • Usability study: I ran a study with 13 older adults on the early prototype, the basis for our paper.
  • Focus groups: the user-testing team ran its own sessions with older adults, separately, to test the courses.
  • Workshop pilots: 10+ sessions with more than 100 older adults, using the courses.
  • Editorial review: a professional editor reviewed all course content before release.

Documented in a peer-reviewed paper. Accepted by Computers in Human Behavior in November 2026 and forthcoming; I am its second author. Participants in its usability study found the prototype accessible, engaging, and easy to use, and worth passing on to others in their networks.

What it doesn’t show: it tested an early prototype, not the live site. Its 13 participants were mostly highly educated and already familiar with scams. It did not measure learning outcomes.

Key decisions

Screenshots are from the live site, November 2026.

Practice responding to scams, not just reading about them

I designed scenario-based practice so learners could apply the warning signs and response strategies introduced in the lessons.

In Romance & Friendship Scams, learners help a friend decide how to respond to someone she met on Facebook. They read a simulated chat, make choices, and receive feedback. One decision asks whether she should check his profile before continuing the conversation; choosing to check brings feedback that his profile is sparse, with one photo, despite his claim to travel the world. Other courses use different formats, including an audio call from someone posing as a grandson.

Trade-off

Scenario-based practice requires additional dialogue writing, interaction design, and feedback for different choices. It offers a place to practice safely, but cannot fully reproduce the emotional pressure of a real scam.

Teach what to do, not only what to watch for

I structured warning-sign lessons around practical responses: explain the tactic, show what to do, and provide suggested wording where useful. For example, the grandparent-scam lesson pairs urgency as a warning sign with guidance to end the call and verify the story with family members.

Some lessons also include an audio example call and transcript, so learners can hear how the tactic sounds. The aim is to give learners a concrete response they can use, rather than leaving them to work out an action from a general warning.

Trade-off

Action guidance and audio examples add content and production work. I kept each warning sign on its own short page to break the material into manageable steps, at the cost of more pages to navigate.

Make courses easy to use without talking down to learners

The courses use plain language, large type, one idea per screen, and consistent navigation. Prototype testing showed that usability also depended on how learners felt represented.

Our early illustrations showed mostly gray-haired people. One participant over 65 did not identify as old and felt that this made the product seem intended for someone else. We broadened the ages represented in the artwork. Another participant valued not being “treated as if we are morons just because we are over 70.” This became a review principle: provide clear guidance while respecting learners’ experience and judgment.

Design tension

Learners differed in how much guidance they wanted. The challenge was to provide reassurance without assuming that everyone needed the same level of help.

Usage after funding was cut. Funding was cut in December 2025, one month after launch and a year earlier than planned. In October 2026, the site still recorded usage, with no promotion during that reporting period.

Requests from potential adopters. During conference demonstrations before funding was cut, senior centers requested access to the existing courses. A university instructor teaching a course on aging also requested the materials. These requests indicated interest in using the product beyond individual online learning.

What remains to evaluate. These figures document delivery, usage, and interest. They do not establish course completion rates, repeat use, or learning outcomes. Completion and return visits can come from the analytics. For learning outcomes, I designed an outcome study: a pre-test and post-test around each course, and a follow-up phone interview one month later to check what learners retained. It has not been run yet.

What I learned

A learning product needs a delivery model. Senior centers requested the courses, and a participant who taught technology offered to introduce them at his center. These conversations showed that educators and community workers were part of the product’s delivery: they could organize sessions and support learners. When my role ended in December 2025, no one had been named to carry these conversations forward, and after funding was cut they lapsed. Before leaving, I should have assigned each lead an owner and a next step. I learned that partner commitments, teaching support, and responsibility for follow-through belong in the product plan from the start.

Age alone is not enough to guide design. Prototype testing showed that people over 65 differed in how they saw themselves, how comfortable they were with technology, and how much reassurance they wanted. The “older adults” label alone didn’t tell us how much guidance any one learner wanted. I learned to consider identity, technology experience, and support needs separately, and to provide guidance without making assumptions about ability.

Define the evidence needed to judge success. Interviews and usability testing helped us improve the experience. Post-launch analytics showed that people were using the site. We still lacked evidence of whether learners became better at recognizing and responding to scams. I learned to distinguish delivery, usage, usability, and learning outcomes when evaluating progress. Low-pressure evaluations for learners and a separate plan to assess the product’s effectiveness can work together.

What I’d do next

If the project resumed, I would prioritize:

  • Turn existing interest into a partner pilot. Reconnect with the senior centers that requested the courses, agree on a delivery owner and schedule, and adapt workshop materials to their teaching format.
  • Run the outcome study I designed. Use the planned pre-test, post-test, and one-month follow-up call with a broader mix of learners to evaluate changes in scam recognition and response, ideally with learners from the partner pilot.
  • Bring completed content into the live product. Start with the two deepfake mini-courses already developed.
  • Test optional step-by-step guidance. Assess whether additional confirmation helps learners who want more support, while keeping it optional for those comfortable proceeding independently.
